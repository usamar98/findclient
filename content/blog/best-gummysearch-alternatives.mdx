## What happened to GummySearch and when does access end?

GummySearch closed new signups and purchases at the end of November 2025. Its published plan schedules final shutdown and account-data deletion for December 1, 2026. On this guide's publication date, November 23, eligible existing users are still in the transition period. [GummySearch closure announcement](https://gummysearch.com/closing-time/) and [published closure timeline](https://gummysearch.com/docs/gummysearch-is-now-closed-6533h)

Access during the transition depends on the account. The maker describes remaining paid access through the applicable billing period, limited export access for free accounts, and an end date for lifetime customers. Check your account while the service is available and download the material you are entitled to retain. Open the export and confirm that the material you need is actually present before treating the migration as finished.

A collection of bookmarks often contains decisions the URLs cannot explain. Perhaps you saved a post because the author used an unfamiliar name for a product problem. Perhaps three discussions convinced you to drop an idea. Alongside the export, write one paragraph describing what the collection helped you decide. That paragraph is a practical brief for testing a replacement.

## Which part of GummySearch did you actually use?

List the last three decisions you made using the product and identify the input each decision required. Historical research, alert monitoring, and replying to a live request involve different inputs. A tool can be strong at one and a poor fit for another.

A product researcher might ask whether bookkeeping software buyers repeatedly mention a problem over several months. That calls for enough history to inspect examples across time. A developer looking for this week's paid migrations wants fresh requests, the original context, and an efficient way to decide whether to respond. A marketing team following its own brand might care more about routing a complaint to the right person than about either research task.

Turn each need into a question you can score. For historical research, ask whether you can retrieve the original discussions behind a theme. For monitoring, ask how quickly a relevant result reaches the person doing the work. For freelance opportunities, ask how many minutes it takes to go from a match to a credible, manually reviewed reply. Write down which capability would make the purchase pointless if it were missing.

Pick two candidates based on your primary task. A short comparison with real queries is easier to interpret than five trials run on different keywords over different weeks. Use vendor pages to establish advertised capabilities and your trial to establish fit.

## How would a freelancer run a fair comparison?

Write a small test brief before opening the tools. Include a service you deliver, examples of buyer wording, a time window, and the types of result you intend to discard. Test the candidates against the same brief and preserve the links that support your judgment.

For example, imagine a developer who fixes product variant displays on Shopify. Their brief might identify mentions of wrong photos, stale prices, and unavailable sizes. It could exclude course recommendations and people advertising their own services. The developer wants posts from merchants with a current problem, plus enough context to explain one diagnostic step. A broad “Shopify” alert tells you little about this specific job.

Select a few previously known discussions as reference examples. Ask whether the tool can retrieve historical matches before treating a missed old example as a failure. When a service monitors new activity, test it prospectively across the same dates as the others. Do not publish invented requests to manufacture a result. A small sample with no matches is an observation worth keeping.

For each result, record the source URL, discovery time, publication time, and your decision. Mark it relevant if it meets the written brief even when you personally cannot take the work that week. Then record whether the thread is open, whether commercial offers are welcome, and whether anyone has already provided a complete answer. Those are separate reasons a relevant match might never become a sales conversation.

## How can you compare costs without trusting a headline price?

Calculate the cost of reviewing enough relevant examples to do your particular job. Include the subscription or credits, the time spent reading irrelevant alerts, and any second tool required to finish the workflow. Use actual time records from your trial where possible.

Suppose one service produces thirty alerts and six useful discussions in a week. Another produces ten alerts and five useful discussions. The first returns one more useful example, while the second may require fewer review minutes. If the missing discussion concerns your largest customer, the first could still be worth the extra work. If you are a solo freelancer with one short review session per day, the second could fit your working week better. These are illustrative numbers.

Ask about source coverage, historical access, delivery methods, account limits, and what happens when usage runs out. For a credit product, distinguish a charge for scoring a candidate from a charge for generating a response. For a team tool, confirm who can receive alerts and who needs a paid seat to open the context. Record the date and plan beside your findings because products change.

## How do you migrate the valuable parts of an audience library?

Rebuild one working collection before migrating the rest. Pair every retained research theme with a source link, an observation in your words, and an explanation of when you would use it. A smaller library with context can be easier to use than hundreds of unlabeled links.

Start with a meaningful category such as “merchants scheduling a theme change before peak season.” Read several source discussions and note the phrases people actually used. Keep open questions separate from claims the posts support. Two merchants using similar words suggest a question worth studying. They cannot establish how frequently every merchant encounters the problem.

Next, choose a review rhythm and an owner for the collection. A freelancer might open new matches twice a week and archive solved requests. A product team might review themes monthly and preserve contradictory examples. Try exporting a small sample from the replacement early. Check whether another person can understand the research from that export and whether individual claims still point back to their sources.

## What should you choose if you only need one outcome?

Choose the least complicated workflow that reliably delivers the outcome in your test. A designer who needs three relevant conversations to study may finish the job through manual search. An agency tracking mentions across sources may value alerts and team routing even when each individual search is simple.

Write a one-sentence decision that names the evidence. For example, “We chose this monitor because both account owners could review relevant complaints from our two priority sources within our normal morning check.” A sentence such as “It uses AI” gives the next person little guidance when coverage or pricing changes. Preserve the runner-up's strongest feature as a reason to revisit the decision later.
